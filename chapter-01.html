Chapter One

When Something Doesn’t Feel Right

The first signs of uncertainty, and the questions that follow.

There is a particular kind of uncertainty that can enter a relationship when something no longer feels the way it once did. It does not always begin with an argument, a disagreement, or a major disappointment. Sometimes it begins quietly, through small moments that leave a person wondering whether something has changed.

You may still love the person standing beside you. You may still enjoy their company, look forward to seeing them, and imagine a future together. You may even recognize the ways they are trying to make the relationship work. Yet something inside you continues to ask questions that you cannot seem to put to rest.

You begin noticing things you might not have paid much attention to before. You notice how often someone looks at their phone, how they respond when you speak, whether they initiate a conversation, and whether the time you spend together feels meaningful. You may find yourself remembering how things used to be and comparing those memories with what is happening now. Before long, ordinary moments begin carrying emotional weight that neither person may fully understand.

The difficult part is that the feeling itself can be real even when the explanation behind it is not yet clear.

A person who feels neglected may genuinely be experiencing loneliness, but that does not automatically mean their partner has stopped caring. Someone who feels questioned may genuinely feel pressured, but that does not necessarily mean their partner is trying to control everything. Both people can be responding to something they believe is happening, even when they have not taken the time to discover whether they understand the situation correctly.

This is where many relationships begin to struggle. Instead of bringing their concerns into an honest conversation, people sometimes begin building conclusions around what they observe. They interpret a look, assign meaning to a silence, or assume they understand why something happened. The longer those assumptions remain unspoken, the more difficult it can become to separate what actually happened from what each person believes it meant.

Consider two people spending an evening together at home. One person is sitting nearby, scrolling through a phone, while the other is hoping for conversation and attention. The person on the phone may not realize that their behavior is creating distance. They may believe that simply being together is enough, especially if they have spent the day working, handling responsibilities, and trying to manage everything else in their lives.

The other person may see the same moment very differently. They may feel that their presence is being overlooked and wonder why someone who says they love them seems more interested in a screen than in sharing time together. What begins as disappointment can become frustration, especially when the person does not know how to explain what they need without sounding demanding.

Eventually, one of them may say, “You’re always on your phone.”

The other may respond, “I’m just sitting here. What’s the problem?”

Those two statements reveal more than a disagreement about a phone. One person is trying to communicate a feeling of disconnection, while the other is responding to what sounds like criticism. Neither response necessarily addresses what is happening beneath the surface.

The conversation can quickly move away from the original concern. Instead of discussing the need for attention, they begin arguing about whether the accusation is fair. One feels unheard, while the other feels unfairly judged. By the time the conversation ends, both may be more frustrated than they were before it began.

This is how a small moment can become a much larger problem when people respond to the words they hear without understanding the feelings behind them.

It is important, however, to recognize that wanting attention is not unreasonable. People need connection, affection, consideration, and communication in their relationships. Wanting to feel valued does not make someone overly sensitive, and asking for meaningful time together should not automatically be treated as a complaint. At the same time, expecting another person to recognize every unspoken need can create disappointment because no one can consistently respond to expectations they have never been given an opportunity to understand.

The challenge is learning how to communicate those needs without turning them into accusations, and learning how to receive those concerns without immediately treating them as an attack.

Sometimes the discomfort we experience in a relationship is connected to something that happened long before the current relationship began. Previous disappointments can shape the way we interpret new experiences, especially when we have promised ourselves that we will never allow someone to hurt us in the same way again.

A person who has experienced betrayal may become especially alert to anything that resembles dishonesty. Someone who has felt abandoned may become sensitive to changes in attention or communication. A person who has spent years feeling unheard may become frustrated when they believe they are being ignored, even when the other person has not recognized what their silence communicates.

These reactions do not always happen consciously. A person may genuinely believe they are responding only to the situation in front of them, without realizing how much of their response is influenced by an earlier experience.

The desire to protect oneself can become so strong that ordinary uncertainty begins to feel threatening. Instead of allowing a conversation to unfold, someone may begin searching for evidence that confirms their fears. They may ask the same question repeatedly, revisit an earlier disagreement, or struggle to accept reassurance because the fear of being disappointed has become louder than the possibility that things may be different this time.

Overthinking often grows in that space between what a person knows and what they fear. The mind tries to fill in missing information, but the conclusions it creates are not always accurate. A delayed response becomes a reason to question someone’s feelings. A quiet evening becomes evidence that something is wrong. An unexpected change in plans becomes a reminder of every previous disappointment.

The person experiencing these thoughts may not be trying to create conflict. They may be trying to prevent another painful experience, but the methods they use to feel secure can sometimes place strain on the very relationship they hope to protect.

This is where honest self examination becomes necessary. We have to ask whether we are responding to the person in front of us or reacting to someone who hurt us in the past. We have to consider whether our expectations are being communicated clearly or whether we are expecting another person to understand feelings we have not fully explained ourselves.

Recognizing these patterns does not mean dismissing legitimate concerns. A person’s history should never be used to invalidate their feelings, and not every concern is simply a result of overthinking. Sometimes something really is wrong, and a person deserves the opportunity to address it. The goal is not to question every emotion until nothing feels trustworthy. The goal is to understand where the emotion comes from so that our response reflects the present situation rather than being controlled entirely by the past.

Another difficult part of relationships is that people do not always measure effort in the same way.

One person may believe they are showing love by making plans, helping with responsibilities, working toward financial stability, or being available when needed. The other may appreciate those actions but still long for more affection, conversation, reassurance, or emotional closeness. Neither person necessarily has an unreasonable expectation, but they may be overlooking the fact that love can be expressed and received differently.

This becomes especially complicated when life circumstances limit what a couple can do together.

When someone is living under restrictions that require advance planning, even ordinary activities can involve more preparation than another person might realize. A spontaneous dinner, a trip to the store, or an afternoon outdoors may require permission, scheduling, and careful attention to established conditions. Those limitations can affect privacy, independence, and the freedom to make everyday decisions.

A partner may understand those circumstances intellectually and still struggle emotionally with the limitations they create. They may miss the freedom to make plans without considering a schedule or wish that their relationship could look more like the relationships they see around them. Recognizing that frustration does not mean they fail to appreciate the other person’s efforts.

Likewise, the person living with those restrictions may feel that their efforts are being overlooked when they have to arrange everything in advance. They may believe that making time together possible demonstrates commitment, especially when their circumstances make ordinary activities more complicated.

Both experiences deserve to be heard.

The danger comes when one person uses their effort as proof that the other should not have anything to complain about, while the other uses their disappointment as proof that the effort does not matter. Once that happens, appreciation and unmet needs become competing arguments rather than subjects that can be discussed together.

A relationship cannot grow by keeping a record of who has done more. It grows when both people can recognize what is being offered while remaining honest about what is still missing.

When something does not feel right, it is easy to focus entirely on the behavior that first caught our attention. We may ask why someone is always on their phone, why they seem distant, why they do not initiate conversations, or why they respond defensively whenever a concern is raised.

Those questions may be important, but there is often another question beneath them.

What am I actually needing in this moment?

Perhaps the need is reassurance. Perhaps it is affection, consistency, honesty, attention, or the feeling that someone is genuinely interested in sharing life together. Sometimes the need is simply to be heard without having to defend why something matters.

Learning to identify that need can change the direction of a conversation. Instead of saying, “You never pay attention to me,” a person might explain, “I miss spending time with you when we are together, and I would like us to put our phones away for a while so we can enjoy each other.”

The second statement does not guarantee that the conversation will go perfectly, but it gives the other person a clearer understanding of what is being requested. It creates an opportunity to respond to a need rather than defend against an accusation.

The same responsibility applies to the person receiving the concern. Instead of immediately responding with frustration, they can pause long enough to consider why the other person feels disconnected. They may not agree with every interpretation, but they can still acknowledge that the feeling deserves attention.

Understanding does not require agreement on every detail. It requires a willingness to take another person’s experience seriously while remaining honest about your own.

Before deciding that something is wrong with another person, it is worth examining what is happening within ourselves. That examination should not become an excuse for someone else’s behavior, but it can help us recognize the difference between a genuine concern, an unmet expectation, and a fear that has followed us from another season of life.

Ask yourself whether you have clearly communicated what you need, whether you have given the other person a fair opportunity to respond, and whether you are interpreting their actions through experiences they may know nothing about. Consider whether you are listening to understand or listening only for something that confirms what you already believe.

Then consider the other side. Are you showing the kind of attention and consideration you expect to receive? Are you dismissing concerns because you do not understand them? Are you assuming that your intentions should be enough, even when your actions are creating a different experience for someone else?

These questions are not about finding someone to blame. They are about making room for responsibility on both sides.

A relationship does not become healthy simply because two people love each other. Love must be accompanied by honesty, consideration, communication, and the willingness to change behavior when that behavior causes harm. Neither person should have to surrender their sense of self to keep the relationship together, and neither should be expected to carry the entire responsibility for making it work.

Sometimes something does not feel right because an important conversation has not happened. Sometimes it is because two people have different expectations that need to be discussed. Sometimes it is because old wounds are influencing present reactions, and sometimes it is because behavior needs to change.

The only way to begin understanding the difference is to stop treating every uncomfortable feeling as a complete explanation.

The feeling may be telling you that something needs attention, but it does not always tell you exactly what needs to change.

That is where the conversation must begin, because what remains unspoken today can become the argument that neither person knows how to resolve tomorrow.
