Chapter Three

Loving Someone You Don’t Understand

Love is a beginning. Understanding takes continued effort.

One of the hardest things to accept in a relationship is that loving someone does not automatically mean understanding them.

We often enter relationships believing that love will naturally bring us closer, help us understand one another, and make it easier to navigate the difficult moments that eventually come. We believe that when two people genuinely care about each other, they should be able to recognize what the other person needs and find a way to work through their differences.

But love does not always make understanding easy.

Two people can share a home, spend most of their free time together, make plans for the future, and still struggle to understand what is happening inside one another. They can know each other’s routines, preferences, favorite places, and daily responsibilities while remaining unfamiliar with the thoughts and emotions that influence how they respond to certain situations.

Sometimes we know someone well enough to recognize their habits but not well enough to understand what those habits mean to them.

One person may need conversation to feel connected, while the other feels most comfortable showing affection through actions. One may want to discuss a disagreement immediately because unresolved tension creates anxiety, while the other needs time to process what happened before attempting to talk. Neither approach automatically means that one person loves more than the other. They may simply have different ways of responding to emotional experiences.

The difficulty begins when those differences become personal.

A request for more conversation may be interpreted as criticism. A need for quiet may be interpreted as rejection. A desire for reassurance may sound like distrust, while an attempt to explain one’s intentions may sound like an excuse.

What one person considers an expression of love may not be what the other person recognizes as love.

Two People, Two Different Experiences

Imagine a couple who have spent the entire week working around responsibilities, schedules, and the demands of everyday life. They have limited opportunities to enjoy uninterrupted time together, but they are looking forward to spending an evening with one another.

One person has been thinking about how nice it would be to sit together, talk, laugh, and enjoy the evening without distractions. The other has been looking forward to finally relaxing after a demanding week.

When they are together, one person reaches for their phone and begins scrolling through messages. They are comfortable, perhaps unaware that anything is wrong. The other person notices and begins to feel disappointed.

At first, nothing is said. The disappointment settles in quietly, and the evening continues. Eventually, the person who wanted more attention begins responding differently. Their answers become shorter, their expression changes, and the warmth they expected to feel gives way to frustration.

The person on the phone notices the change but does not understand where it came from.

“What’s wrong?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re acting like something is wrong.”

“Well, I just wanted to spend some time with you.”

The response may be, “We are spending time together.”

And there it is. Two people are sitting in the same room, sharing the same evening, but they are experiencing that evening differently.

One believes being together should be enough to communicate connection. The other believes meaningful time together requires attention and participation. Neither may have considered that their understanding of quality time is different.

The disagreement is not necessarily about the phone. It is about what each person believes it means to be present.

One may feel accused for simply relaxing. The other may feel unimportant because the attention they hoped for never arrived. If they continue arguing about whether someone was technically present, they may never address the emotional need that started the disagreement.

Understanding requires looking beyond the immediate behavior and considering what that behavior represents to each person.

Love Is Not the Same as Understanding

There are people who genuinely love one another but have never learned how to communicate in ways the other person can receive.

They may express affection through practical support, financial responsibility, physical closeness, or making time for shared activities. Their partner may appreciate those things while still needing verbal encouragement, emotional reassurance, or more consistent communication.

This does not mean that one person’s expression of love is invalid. It means that love must be accompanied by a willingness to understand how it is experienced by someone else.

A person may say, “I do everything I can for you. What more do you want?”

The question may come from genuine frustration. They may believe they have demonstrated their commitment through everything they have done. Yet the person hearing those words may feel that their emotional needs are being treated as an inconvenience.

Both people can be telling the truth about their experiences. One feels that their efforts are overlooked, while the other feels that their needs are not being recognized.

The answer cannot simply be that one should appreciate what is being offered and stop asking for anything more. Nor can it be that the other must constantly prove their love by meeting every expectation. A healthy relationship requires both people to understand that effort and emotional connection are not competing responsibilities.

It is possible to appreciate what someone does while still communicating what you need. It is also possible to feel unappreciated while recognizing that the other person may be asking for something you have not fully understood.

Love should create a reason to learn about one another, not an excuse to stop trying.

The Difference Between Intention and Impact

One of the most important distinctions in relationships is the difference between what someone intended to do and how their actions affected another person.

A person may not intend to hurt their partner when they become distracted during a conversation. They may not intend to create distance when they need time alone, and they may not realize that a particular response sounds dismissive. Their intentions may be completely different from what the other person experiences.

But good intentions do not automatically erase the impact of behavior.

If someone repeatedly feels ignored, telling them that no harm was intended may not address the loneliness they are experiencing. If someone feels constantly criticized, explaining that the other person only wants reassurance may not resolve the pressure they feel.

Both perspectives matter, and neither should be used to silence the other.

Consider how different a conversation might become when someone responds with curiosity rather than immediately defending their intentions.

Instead of saying, “I wasn’t doing anything wrong,” they might ask, “Can you help me understand what bothered you about that?”

Instead of saying, “You never care about what I need,” the other person might explain, “When we spend time together and you are distracted, I start feeling disconnected from you.”

Those responses do not guarantee agreement, but they create room for a conversation that addresses the actual experience rather than turning the disagreement into a judgment of someone’s character.

Understanding another person’s feelings does not require accepting every interpretation as fact. It requires being willing to consider that your actions may have affected them in ways you did not anticipate.

That willingness can change the entire direction of a relationship.

When Circumstances Complicate Connection

Relationships do not develop in isolation from the circumstances surrounding them. Work schedules, financial responsibilities, family obligations, living arrangements, legal restrictions, and personal struggles can all influence how much freedom two people have and how they spend their time.

Sometimes a couple wants to do ordinary things together but must plan around circumstances that other couples may never have to consider.

A dinner out may require advance arrangements. A weekend activity may involve coordinating schedules and responsibilities. Even something as simple as deciding to go somewhere together may require more preparation than either person would prefer.

Those circumstances can create frustration, especially when one person longs for greater freedom and the other is already doing everything possible within the restrictions they face.

The person who wants more spontaneity may begin to feel that their relationship is missing experiences they wish they could share. The person managing the restrictions may feel discouraged when their efforts to make those experiences possible are not recognized.

It is important to understand that both feelings can exist without either person being unreasonable.

Circumstances explain some limitations, but they do not eliminate the need for communication. A person can have genuine restrictions and still show consideration. Another person can understand those restrictions and still express disappointment. Neither should be expected to pretend that the situation is effortless.

When two people are working toward a shared future, they must learn how to recognize the effort being made in the present without ignoring the needs that remain unmet.

The future they hope to build should give them something to work toward, but it should not become a reason to dismiss everything they are experiencing today.

The Danger of Assuming You Know Someone

Familiarity can sometimes make us less curious about the people we love.

We begin believing that we already know why someone responds a certain way. We recognize their habits, remember previous disagreements, and develop expectations about how they will react. Eventually, we may stop asking questions because we believe we already know the answers.

When that happens, we can begin responding to an assumption instead of the person standing in front of us.

A person who has withdrawn during previous disagreements may be expected to withdraw again. Someone who has become defensive in the past may be approached as though they are already preparing to argue. Another person who has overthought situations before may have their concerns dismissed before they have even finished explaining them.

The past can provide useful information, but it should not become a permanent substitute for listening.

People are capable of changing. They can learn new ways to communicate, recognize unhealthy patterns, and become more considerate of the people they love. However, change must be demonstrated through consistent behavior rather than promises alone.

Understanding someone means allowing room for growth while remaining honest about patterns that continue to cause harm.

It also means recognizing that we may have misunderstood them before. Perhaps we assigned an intention they never had. Perhaps we interpreted their silence incorrectly, or perhaps we never gave them the opportunity to explain what they were experiencing.

Being willing to reconsider an assumption is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign that understanding matters more than protecting an opinion.

When Understanding Becomes One Sided

There is another important part of this conversation that cannot be overlooked.

Sometimes one person spends so much time trying to understand the other that they begin neglecting their own emotional well-being. They explain away repeated disappointments, make excuses for behavior that hurts them, and convince themselves that patience means accepting whatever happens.

That is not understanding. That is losing sight of your own needs.

It is possible to recognize why someone behaves a certain way without agreeing that the behavior is acceptable. It is possible to understand someone’s history without becoming responsible for healing every wound they carry. It is possible to love someone while recognizing that the relationship requires changes they must be willing to make.

Understanding should never become a requirement to tolerate intimidation, manipulation, humiliation, or abuse.

The responsibility to communicate, listen, and change must be shared. If one person is always adjusting while the other refuses to acknowledge their behavior, the relationship is not developing mutual understanding.

There must be room for both people to be heard, both people to grow, and both people to take responsibility.

Learning the Person Behind the Behavior

Sometimes we become so focused on what someone does that we forget to learn why certain things matter to them.

We may know that someone becomes quiet during conflict without understanding what makes confrontation difficult for them. We may know that someone needs reassurance without understanding what experiences have shaped that need. We may recognize that someone becomes frustrated when plans change without understanding how much uncertainty affects them.

Learning the person behind the behavior requires more than observing their reactions. It requires asking thoughtful questions, listening to the answers, and being willing to recognize experiences that may be different from our own.

It also requires honesty about the things we discover.

Not every difference can be resolved through compromise, and not every expectation will align. Some differences require patience and adjustment, while others reveal incompatibilities that deserve serious consideration. The purpose of understanding is not to guarantee that every relationship will continue. It is to make sure that decisions are based on a clearer picture of what is happening rather than assumptions and incomplete conversations.

You cannot truly understand someone if you are only interested in the parts of their experience that agree with your own.

Looking Beneath the Surface

Ask yourself whether you understand how the person you love experiences connection, communication, affection, and conflict. Do you know what makes them feel appreciated, or have you assumed that what makes you feel loved should be enough for them?

When something bothers you, do you become curious about their intentions, or do you immediately decide what their behavior means? When someone expresses a need that you do not share, can you recognize its importance without treating it as an unreasonable expectation?

And perhaps the most difficult question is this: are you making an honest effort to understand the other person while allowing yourself to be understood in return?

These questions matter because relationships are not sustained by affection alone. They require two people who are willing to keep learning about one another, especially when their differences become uncomfortable.

The person you love may not always understand why something matters so deeply to you. You may not always understand why they respond the way they do. That does not mean the relationship is without love, but it does mean that love needs the support of communication, patience, accountability, and consistent effort.

We cannot expect someone to understand everything we have never explained, and we cannot expect ourselves to understand someone simply because we have spent years beside them.

Sometimes we have to slow down, ask another question, listen to an unfamiliar perspective, and accept that the person we thought we understood may have been carrying an experience we never fully recognized.

Loving someone is an important beginning, but understanding them is an ongoing responsibility.

And when two people begin to recognize that they are not always experiencing the same relationship in the same way, they may finally become willing to have the conversations they have been avoiding.

Because the greatest test is not whether two people can avoid disagreement. It is whether they can remain willing to understand one another when disagreement finally comes.
