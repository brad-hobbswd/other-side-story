Chapter Fourteen

Choosing Understanding Over Winning

Moving beyond the need to win and making room for connection.

There is something about an argument that can make us forget why the conversation started in the first place.

We may begin by trying to explain something that hurt us, hoping the other person will understand. But as the conversation continues, our attention shifts. We become focused on defending our position, correcting their version of events, and making sure they understand exactly why we believe we are right.

Before long, the original concern has become secondary.

Now we are arguing about the words that were used, who started the disagreement, who misunderstood whom, and who owes the other person an apology.

We may even leave the conversation feeling satisfied because we made our point, only to realize later that nothing between us has actually improved.

The disagreement may be over, but the distance remains.

This is one of the difficult realities of relationships: winning an argument does not necessarily mean we have made progress.

Sometimes we leave a conversation having proved our point and lost an opportunity to connect.

When Being Right Becomes More Important Than Being Heard

Most people want to feel that their perspective matters.

We want the other person to recognize what happened, acknowledge how it affected us, and understand why we responded the way we did. When we feel misunderstood, we may repeat ourselves because we believe that if we can just explain it one more time, the other person will finally see what we have been trying to communicate.

But repetition does not always create understanding.

Sometimes it creates frustration.

Imagine one person saying, “You never make time for me.”

The other responds, “That is not true. We went out last weekend.”

The first person explains that they are not talking about one particular outing. They are talking about feeling emotionally disconnected during the ordinary moments they share at home.

The second person continues listing the activities they have planned, believing that those examples prove they are making an effort.

Neither person is necessarily lying about their experience.

One is describing a feeling of disconnection. The other is defending the effort they believe should demonstrate care.

But the conversation has become a debate over the word “never” rather than an opportunity to understand what the first person is actually missing.

This is how people can spend an entire evening arguing without ever addressing the original concern.

Sometimes we become so determined to correct the other person’s wording that we fail to hear the meaning behind it.

That does not mean words are unimportant. Accusations should be addressed, and exaggerated statements can make honest conversations more difficult. But correcting every imperfect expression should not become a way to avoid the concern itself.

We can say, “I do not think it is fair to say I never make time for you, but I want to understand why you are feeling disconnected.”

That response protects our perspective while still making room for theirs.

The Need to Have the Last Word

Some disagreements continue long after both people have run out of anything meaningful to say.

One person makes a comment. The other responds. Another comment follows, then another correction, another explanation, and another attempt to make sure the conversation ends with the right words.

Neither wants to be the one who stops responding.

Silence may feel like surrender. Walking away may feel like admitting defeat. Acknowledging the other person’s point may feel like giving them control over the entire disagreement.

So the conversation continues.

But what are we actually trying to accomplish?

If the goal is to understand one another, the last word should not matter nearly as much as what happens after the conversation ends.

There are moments when continuing to argue does more harm than good. When emotions are high, people may begin saying things they would not otherwise say. They may bring up unrelated mistakes, make personal attacks, or use private information as ammunition.

At that point, the conversation is no longer serving its original purpose.

Sometimes the healthiest response is to pause.

That does not mean ignoring the issue or refusing to finish the discussion. It means recognizing when the conversation has stopped being productive and agreeing to return to it when both people can communicate more thoughtfully.

There is no shame in saying, “I want to work this out, but I do not think we are hearing each other right now. Can we take a little time and come back to this?”

Choosing to pause is different from using silence to punish someone. The intention should be to return to the conversation, not to leave the other person wondering whether the issue will ever be addressed.

Understanding Is Not the Same as Agreeing

One reason people struggle to listen is that they believe understanding another person’s perspective means accepting it as correct.

It does not.

We can understand why someone feels disappointed without agreeing with every conclusion they have reached. We can recognize that a person felt ignored without agreeing that we intentionally ignored them. We can acknowledge that someone experienced a situation differently without pretending our own experience did not happen.

Understanding is an effort to recognize another person’s point of view.

Agreement is a conclusion about whether we share that point of view.

Those are two different things.

Consider a couple discussing how they spend their time together.

One person may believe that planning activities around work schedules demonstrates consideration. The other may appreciate those plans but still want more spontaneous moments or meaningful connection at home.

They may not agree on what a satisfying balance looks like.

But they can still understand what each person values.

One may need to recognize that planned time is meaningful. The other may need to recognize that emotional connection cannot be measured only by how many activities are scheduled.

The conversation does not require one person to surrender their perspective. It requires both to become curious about what the other person is trying to communicate.

Sometimes understanding does not lead to agreement. It simply allows two people to make decisions with a clearer picture of one another.

When Every Conversation Feels Like a Trial

There are relationships where ordinary conversations begin to feel like someone is constantly being questioned.

A concern is raised, and the other person immediately begins explaining their actions. One detail is challenged, then another. Before long, both people are presenting evidence to support their version of events.

Who said what?

Who remembered it correctly?

Who did more?

Who apologized first?

Who made the first mistake?

It can begin to feel less like a relationship and more like a courtroom.

There are situations where details matter, especially when someone is trying to establish what happened after trust has been broken. Accuracy should never be sacrificed simply to make a conversation easier.

But not every disagreement needs to become a trial.

Sometimes the real question is much simpler: What are we experiencing, and what needs to change?

When every conversation becomes an attempt to prove innocence, people may become afraid to admit mistakes. They may begin hiding their feelings because they expect those feelings to be challenged rather than heard.

That creates an environment where honesty becomes difficult.

A relationship needs room for people to speak imperfectly, clarify what they mean, acknowledge mistakes, and discuss difficult experiences without feeling that every sentence will be used against them.

That does not mean avoiding accountability. It means creating conversations where accountability can actually happen.

The Difference Between Winning and Resolving

Winning an argument often focuses on the immediate disagreement.

Resolving a problem requires looking at what needs to happen afterward.

A person may win an argument about how much time they spend together, but if their partner continues feeling disconnected, the underlying issue remains.

Someone may successfully defend why they became frustrated, but if their words caused lasting hurt, the relationship still needs repair.

A person may prove that they were technically correct about a particular detail, but that does not automatically restore trust or improve communication.

Resolution asks different questions.

What did we learn from this conversation?

What part of the problem can we address?

What needs to change?

What can each person reasonably contribute?

Are we both willing to follow through?

These questions move the conversation away from personal victory and toward practical improvement.

Sometimes the solution is a change in routine. Sometimes it requires clearer expectations, more honest communication, or a boundary that has not been respected.

And sometimes the conversation reveals that the two people want different things and must decide whether those differences can be worked through.

Not every disagreement has an easy resolution, but every meaningful conversation should give us a better understanding of what we are facing.

When One Person Is Always Expected to Give In

Choosing understanding should never become another way of asking one person to surrender.

There is a difference between compromise and repeatedly giving up what matters to you because the other person refuses to consider another perspective.

If one person always has to apologize to end the argument, that is not necessarily peace. If one person constantly changes their expectations while the other makes no effort to adjust, that is not mutual understanding.

A healthy relationship does not require one person to become smaller so the other can remain comfortable.

Both people should have the freedom to express disagreement, raise concerns, and maintain reasonable boundaries.

Sometimes choosing understanding means recognizing that the other person has a legitimate point. Other times it means respectfully standing firm because something important is being overlooked.

We should not confuse being agreeable with being emotionally healthy.

There are moments when the most honest thing we can say is, “I understand why you see it that way, but I cannot agree to that.”

Understanding should make room for truth, not eliminate it.

Learning to Approach Each Other Differently

Imagine returning to a familiar disagreement, but this time both people make a different choice.

Instead of beginning with an accusation, one person explains what they have been feeling.

“I know you make plans for us, and I appreciate that. I think what I am struggling with is feeling connected when we are just at home together.”

The other person listens before responding.

“I did not realize that was what you meant. I thought making time for activities was showing you that I wanted to spend time together.”

The first person acknowledges the effort.

“I know you do. I am not saying you do nothing. I am trying to explain that I need something different sometimes.”

The second person considers the request.

“I can understand that. I also want you to recognize that I have responsibilities and that I am trying to balance everything.”

Now there is room for both perspectives.

They may decide to set aside time without phones. They may agree that planned outings matter while also making space for quieter moments together. They may discover that the solution is not as complicated as the argument made it seem.

The important difference is that neither person had to defeat the other to be heard.

This kind of conversation takes effort. It requires patience, emotional maturity, and a willingness to recognize that the person beside us is not automatically our opponent.

It also requires both people to participate. One person cannot create mutual understanding alone.

When Understanding Reveals Something Difficult

Sometimes listening carefully changes our understanding of the relationship in ways we did not expect.

We may discover that the other person has been feeling unappreciated. We may recognize that we have misunderstood their intentions. We may also discover that certain concerns have been raised repeatedly without meaningful change.

Not every difficult conversation leads to reconciliation.

Sometimes understanding reveals a difference in values, expectations, or willingness to make an effort. Sometimes it shows that a relationship has become unhealthy or that boundaries need to be established more firmly.

That realization can be painful.

But understanding should never require us to ignore what we have learned simply because the truth is uncomfortable.

Choosing understanding over winning does not mean staying in every situation. It means approaching the truth without allowing pride, defensiveness, or the desire for control to determine what we are willing to hear.

There is a difference between fighting to preserve a connection and fighting to preserve an argument.

We must learn to recognize which one we are doing.

Looking Beneath the Surface

Think about the last disagreement in which you felt strongly that you had to prove your point.

Were you trying to resolve the issue, or were you trying to make sure the other person understood that you were right?

Did you listen to what they were saying, or were you preparing your response while they were still talking?

Have you ever continued an argument simply because you did not want the other person to have the last word?

Consider whether you sometimes mistake understanding for agreement. Can you acknowledge another person’s feelings without abandoning your own perspective?

And when you disagree, are you willing to search for a solution that respects both people?

These questions are not about avoiding conflict. Healthy relationships need honest conversations, even when those conversations are uncomfortable.

The goal is to stop treating every disagreement as something that must produce a winner and a loser.

Sometimes the most productive thing we can do is set aside the need to prove our position long enough to understand what the other person is actually trying to say.

The Choice to Move Forward

A relationship cannot grow if every difficult conversation becomes another opportunity to keep score.

There will always be moments when two people disagree. They will have different expectations, different emotional needs, and different ways of responding to stress. They will misunderstand one another, make mistakes, and occasionally say things they wish they had handled differently.

What matters is what happens next.

Can they listen without immediately defending themselves? Can they acknowledge hurt without turning it into a competition? Can they disagree without attacking one another’s character? Can they recognize when a conversation needs to pause and still return to the issue that needs attention?

These are not small things. They are choices that shape the emotional environment of a relationship.

Choosing understanding over winning does not guarantee that two people will remain together. It does not promise that every disagreement will end peacefully or that every person will become willing to change.

But it does offer something valuable: the opportunity to approach one another with honesty instead of hostility.

We cannot build a meaningful connection while constantly trying to defeat the person with whom we are supposed to be building it.

And perhaps the greatest question is not whether we can prove that our side of the story is correct.

It is whether we are willing to listen closely enough to discover what the other side has been trying to tell us.

Because sometimes the relationship does not need another argument. It needs two people willing to make a different choice.

And when that choice is made, the next question becomes even more important: What happens when love itself requires us to change?

Understanding can open the door, but the willingness to change determines whether we walk through it.
