Chapter Nine

When Defending Yourself Becomes a Habit

Making room for accountability without losing your voice.

There are times when someone brings a concern to us, and before we have even understood what they are saying, we begin preparing a defense.

We hear the first few words and immediately feel the need to explain ourselves. We remember what happened, what we intended, and everything the other person may have overlooked. We begin thinking about all the reasons our actions made sense, even before we have given ourselves an opportunity to consider how those actions affected someone else.

Sometimes the response comes so naturally that we do not recognize it as defensiveness.

We may believe we are simply correcting a misunderstanding, protecting our intentions, or making sure the other person knows the entire story. And sometimes that is exactly what we need to do. There are situations in which our words or actions have been interpreted incorrectly, and we deserve an opportunity to explain ourselves.

But there is a difference between explaining your perspective and using every conversation as an opportunity to prove that you did nothing wrong.

When defending ourselves becomes a habit, we can become so focused on protecting our position that we stop recognizing what the other person is trying to communicate.

We may hear a concern as an accusation, a request as criticism, or a disagreement as a personal attack. Instead of considering whether there is something we need to acknowledge, we begin searching for reasons why the other person should not be upset.

Over time, this pattern can make honest communication increasingly difficult.

When Every Conversation Feels Like an Attack

Imagine someone approaching their partner with a concern.

“I wanted to talk to you about something that bothered me yesterday.”

Before the person has an opportunity to explain, the response comes quickly.

“What did I do now?”

The question may sound harmless to the person asking it, but it can communicate frustration before the conversation has even begun. The person bringing up the concern may immediately feel that they are already being treated as a problem.

They continue anyway.

“When we were talking yesterday, I felt like you weren’t really listening to me.”

The response comes just as quickly.

“I was listening. I was just tired.”

Now the conversation is focused on whether the person was technically listening rather than why the other person felt disconnected.

The person bringing up the concern may try to explain further, but the response becomes increasingly defensive.

“So now I can’t even be tired?”

That was never the point.

One person wanted to discuss a moment when they felt overlooked. The other heard an accusation that they were not doing enough. Both have become frustrated, and the original concern is once again lost.

This pattern can develop when someone begins approaching every complaint as though it is a judgment of their entire character.

They may struggle to recognize that a person can disagree with something they did without believing they are a bad person. They may hear disappointment and assume rejection. They may hear a request for change and interpret it as a declaration that everything they have done is meaningless.

But a concern does not have to be a condemnation.

Sometimes someone is simply trying to tell us that something affected them, and the most helpful response is to understand what happened before deciding how to defend ourselves.

The Difference Between an Explanation and an Excuse

There is nothing wrong with explaining your actions.

People deserve to be understood, and circumstances can provide important information about why something happened. If you were distracted because of work, overwhelmed by responsibilities, or dealing with something personally difficult, those details may help another person understand your behavior.

However, an explanation becomes an excuse when it is used to avoid acknowledging the impact of what happened.

Consider someone who repeatedly arrives late to plans with their partner.

They may have legitimate reasons. Work may run longer than expected, traffic may create delays, or unexpected responsibilities may interfere with the schedule. Explaining those circumstances is reasonable.

But if their partner repeatedly expresses disappointment, responding with another explanation without acknowledging the inconvenience may leave the underlying concern unresolved.

The issue is no longer only why someone was late. It is also whether they recognize that another person has been waiting, adjusting their schedule, and feeling that their time has not been considered.

A person can explain what happened and still acknowledge the impact.

They might say, “I know I have been late several times, and I understand why that frustrates you. Work has been unpredictable, but I should have communicated sooner when I knew I was running behind.”

That response does not require someone to accept blame for circumstances beyond their control. It recognizes the part of the situation they can take responsibility for.

This is an important distinction because accountability is not about accepting responsibility for everything that goes wrong. It is about honestly recognizing the part we played and being willing to address it.

When Pride Gets in the Way

Sometimes defensiveness is connected to pride.

Admitting that we were wrong can feel uncomfortable, especially when we believe we have tried hard to do the right thing. We may worry that acknowledging a mistake will make us look weak, give the other person an advantage, or cause them to believe that every criticism they have made is correct.

As a result, we may argue over small details rather than acknowledge the larger issue.

We may correct someone’s wording, question their memory, or bring up something they did wrong because admitting our own mistake feels too difficult.

The conversation becomes a competition over who has made more mistakes instead of an opportunity to recognize what needs to change.

Pride can make an apology feel like defeat, even when an apology could be the beginning of repairing something important.

There is strength in being able to say, “I did not handle that well.”

There is maturity in recognizing that our intentions were good but our response was hurtful. There is growth in accepting that someone else’s experience may reveal something about our behavior that we had not considered before.

Admitting a mistake does not erase everything good about us. It simply recognizes that being a good person does not mean we will always make the right decisions.

The people we love should not have to fight through our pride every time they want to discuss something that hurt them.

When You Feel the Need to Have the Last Word

Another sign that defensiveness has become a habit is the inability to let a conversation end without proving your point.

You may feel compelled to correct the final statement, explain one more detail, or make sure the other person understands why you believe they are wrong.

Even when the original concern has been addressed, you continue explaining because you do not feel satisfied until your perspective has been fully recognized.

Sometimes this comes from a genuine fear of being misunderstood. Other times, it comes from a desire to avoid feeling responsible for something uncomfortable.

Whatever the reason, the need to have the last word can prevent a conversation from reaching a meaningful conclusion.

Imagine two people discussing something that happened during an outing.

One says, “I felt embarrassed when you made that comment in front of everyone.”

The other responds, “I was joking. I didn’t mean anything by it.”

The first person explains that the joke made them uncomfortable. The second insists that everyone else laughed and that nobody appeared offended.

Now the conversation is no longer about how the comment affected the person who heard it. It has become a debate over whether the joke should have been considered embarrassing.

The person who made the comment may never have intended to cause embarrassment, but that does not automatically make the other person’s experience invalid.

Sometimes we need to accept that someone was affected differently than we expected, even when we believe our intentions were harmless.

Not every conversation needs a winner. Sometimes the most meaningful response is recognizing that another person experienced something we did not fully understand.

When You Turn the Concern Around

Defensiveness can become particularly damaging when someone responds to a concern by immediately turning the conversation back onto the person who raised it.

Someone may say, “I felt hurt when you spoke to me that way.”

The response may be, “Well, you speak to me that way all the time.”

Perhaps that is true. Perhaps there is a separate concern that also needs attention. But bringing it up at that moment does not automatically resolve the original issue.

Both concerns may deserve a conversation, but they should not be used to cancel one another out.

This pattern can make people reluctant to speak because they begin expecting that every concern will become an examination of their own behavior.

Instead of feeling heard, they feel placed on trial.

There is a difference between identifying a mutual problem and using someone else’s behavior to avoid responsibility for your own.

A healthy response might be, “I understand that what I said hurt you. I want to talk about that. There is also something I have been struggling with, and I would like us to discuss it afterward.”

That approach allows both people to have a voice without using one person’s concern to silence the other.

Accountability should not be treated as something that can only belong to one person at a time.

When the Person Defending Themselves Feels Misunderstood

It is important to recognize that defensiveness does not always come from arrogance or an unwillingness to care.

Sometimes people become defensive because they genuinely feel misunderstood.

They may believe their actions have been interpreted unfairly, their circumstances have been overlooked, or their efforts have not been recognized. They may feel that the other person has already decided what happened before giving them an opportunity to explain.

That experience can be frustrating.

Someone who feels constantly criticized may begin entering conversations with their guard already raised. They may expect another accusation and respond before they have fully heard what is being said.

Their reaction may be connected to previous experiences in which they felt blamed, judged, or unable to defend themselves.

But even when defensiveness has an understandable origin, it can still prevent healthy communication.

The goal is not to stop explaining yourself. The goal is to learn when an explanation is necessary and when it is preventing you from acknowledging something important.

You can say, “I think there is more to what happened, and I would like to explain my perspective,” while still being willing to hear the other person.

You can disagree with an interpretation without dismissing the feelings behind it.

You can protect yourself from unfair accusations without treating every concern as an attack.

That balance requires self awareness and the willingness to remain open when a conversation becomes uncomfortable.

Accountability Is Not the Same as Taking All the Blame

Some people avoid accountability because they believe admitting one mistake means accepting responsibility for everything that has gone wrong.

They may fear that once they acknowledge their behavior, the other person will use that admission to establish that every previous disagreement was their fault.

This fear can make it difficult to apologize honestly.

But accountability does not require accepting blame that does not belong to you.

You can acknowledge that you raised your voice without agreeing that you were responsible for the entire disagreement. You can apologize for making a hurtful comment without accepting another person’s inaccurate description of your intentions. You can recognize that your behavior needs to change while still maintaining a boundary about how you expect to be treated.

Accountability should be specific and honest.

It asks us to recognize what we did, understand its impact, and consider what we can do differently. It does not require us to surrender our perspective or accept mistreatment.

Likewise, someone receiving an apology should not use it as permission to continue humiliating or punishing the person who offered it.

An apology should create an opportunity for repair, not another opportunity to establish control.

Both people must understand that responsibility and respect belong together.

Learning to Listen Before You Defend

One of the simplest ways to change a defensive pattern is to slow down the response.

When someone brings up a concern, resist the urge to immediately explain why you did what you did. Allow them to finish speaking. Ask questions if something is unclear, and make an effort to understand what they experienced before deciding how to respond.

You might discover that the person is not accusing you of having bad intentions. They may simply be trying to explain that something you did affected them in a way you did not expect.

You may also discover that their interpretation is incomplete or that they have misunderstood part of the situation. There will still be an opportunity to explain your perspective, but the conversation will begin from a place of greater understanding.

It can also help to acknowledge what you heard before offering your explanation.

“I understand that you felt ignored when I was distracted. That was not my intention, but I can see why it bothered you.”

That statement recognizes the other person’s experience without pretending that your intentions did not matter.

The same consideration should be returned. When you express a concern, the other person should be willing to hear your explanation without automatically treating it as an excuse.

Communication becomes healthier when both people can explain themselves without using explanations to avoid accountability.

When Defensiveness Becomes a Wall

Over time, repeated defensiveness can create a wall between two people.

One may stop bringing up concerns because they already know how the conversation will unfold. The other may become accustomed to defending themselves and may not recognize how little room remains for honest discussion.

Eventually, the relationship can become organized around avoiding criticism rather than understanding one another.

People may choose their words carefully, avoid certain subjects, or pretend that something does not bother them because they do not want to trigger another defensive response.

This can create the appearance of peace while important concerns remain unresolved.

The person who avoids difficult conversations may believe they are protecting the relationship. The person who becomes defensive may believe they are protecting themselves. Yet both may be contributing to an environment where honesty becomes increasingly difficult.

A relationship cannot grow when every concern is treated as a threat.

There must be room to admit mistakes, clarify misunderstandings, discuss uncomfortable feelings, and recognize behavior that needs to change.

If someone repeatedly refuses accountability, dismisses concerns, or uses intimidation to prevent discussion, the problem is more serious than an ordinary communication difference. In those situations, the other person may need boundaries and support rather than another attempt to find the perfect words.

Healthy communication requires willingness from both sides.

Looking Beneath the Surface

Consider how you respond when someone tells you that something you did hurt them.

Do you immediately begin explaining yourself, or can you pause long enough to understand what they experienced? Do you feel that acknowledging a mistake will make you look weak, or can you recognize that accountability is part of being a mature person?

Think about whether you sometimes turn a concern around because you feel uncomfortable being the focus of the conversation. Have you ever brought up another person’s mistakes simply because you did not want to acknowledge your own?

If you are the person who feels repeatedly misunderstood, ask whether you are being given a fair opportunity to explain yourself. Are your intentions being considered, or does the conversation become an accusation before you have finished speaking?

It is also worth considering whether you have become so accustomed to defending yourself that you struggle to recognize when someone is offering a genuine opportunity for growth.

The goal is not to become someone who accepts every accusation. It is to become someone who can distinguish between an unfair attack and a concern that deserves attention.

You can stand up for yourself without dismissing someone else’s experience. You can accept responsibility without accepting all the blame. You can explain your intentions while recognizing the impact of your actions.

Most importantly, you can disagree without making the other person feel that honesty will always come at a cost.

The Courage to Admit When You Are Wrong

There is a particular kind of freedom that comes from no longer needing to defend every decision.

When we recognize that mistakes are part of being human, we become more willing to examine our behavior without treating every concern as a threat to our identity.

We can listen without immediately preparing an argument. We can acknowledge that our words were hurtful, even when we were frustrated. We can recognize that our actions created distance, even when we never intended for that to happen.

That kind of honesty does not diminish us. It allows us to grow.

The people we love should not have to convince us repeatedly that their feelings matter. And we should not have to surrender our own experiences simply to make another person comfortable.

A healthy relationship gives both people room to be honest, to make mistakes, to take responsibility, and to demonstrate meaningful change.

The next time someone brings a concern to you, remember that you do not have to agree with every detail to listen with respect. You do not have to accept every accusation to acknowledge an impact. And you do not have to win a disagreement to know that your perspective matters.

Sometimes the strongest response is not another explanation. It is the willingness to stop, listen, and ask yourself whether there is something you need to understand about your own behavior.

Because when defending yourself becomes a habit, you may spend so much time proving that you are not wrong that you never discover what you could have done differently.

And sometimes, the very thing standing between two people and a better relationship is not a lack of love, but the unwillingness to admit that love requires us to keep growing.
