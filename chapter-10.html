Chapter Ten

The Things We Expect but Never Express

The expectations we carry, the conversations we avoid, and the understanding that begins when we speak.

Some of the greatest disappointments in relationships come from expectations that were never spoken aloud.

We may believe that someone should know what matters to us because they love us. We may expect them to recognize when we need attention, understand why a particular situation bothers us, or remember something important without being reminded. We may even become frustrated when they fail to do something that has been on our minds for days, forgetting that the other person may have no idea we were expecting it.

In our minds, the expectation is clear. We know what we wanted, how we hoped the situation would unfold, and what we believe the other person should have understood.

But the person standing beside us may have a completely different understanding.

They may not realize that a particular date is important, that we were hoping they would make the plans, or that a small gesture would have meant so much. They may believe everything is going well because we have never told them otherwise.

When disappointment finally surfaces, they may be surprised to discover that they have failed to meet an expectation they never knew existed.

This is one of the complicated realities of relationships. We sometimes expect people to understand what we have not communicated, and then interpret their lack of understanding as a lack of love.

Imagine someone looking forward to spending a Saturday together.

They have been thinking about it throughout the week. They imagine going somewhere, enjoying a meal, perhaps seeing a movie, or simply having uninterrupted time together. They have not mentioned any specific plans because they are hoping their partner will take the initiative.

Saturday arrives.

The morning passes, and the other person begins discussing ordinary responsibilities. They may mention needing to run errands, finish something around the house, or take care of a few things before the evening.

The person who had been looking forward to the day becomes disappointed.

They may become quieter, respond differently, or begin showing frustration. Eventually, the other person notices.

“What’s wrong?”

“Nothing.”

“Are you sure?”

“I just thought we were going to do something today.”

The response comes with genuine confusion.

“We never talked about making plans.”

And that is when the disappointment becomes an argument.

One person believes the other should have known they wanted to spend time together. The other believes they cannot be expected to understand plans that were never discussed.

Both may feel that their perspective is reasonable.

The person who was disappointed may think, “If you wanted to spend time with me, wouldn’t you have thought about doing something?”

The other may think, “If spending time together was important, why didn’t you tell me?”

The disagreement is no longer simply about Saturday. It is about what each person believes love should naturally recognize.

We often develop ideas about relationships long before we enter them.

We may believe that a loving partner should initiate affection, remember important dates, offer reassurance, make plans, communicate frequently, or recognize when something is bothering us.

Some of those expectations reflect reasonable needs. Others may be influenced by family experiences, previous relationships, personal preferences, or the examples of love we have observed throughout our lives.

The difficulty is that we sometimes assume everyone shares the same understanding.

Someone may believe that making plans together is an important way to demonstrate commitment. Another may believe that being dependable and helping with everyday responsibilities communicates the same commitment.

One person may expect frequent messages throughout the day, while the other believes that a relationship should allow both people to focus on their responsibilities without constant communication.

Neither preference automatically makes someone a better partner.

However, differences become difficult when people treat their own expectations as universal standards rather than recognizing that those expectations need to be discussed.

A person cannot reasonably expect someone else to follow rules they have never communicated. At the same time, a partner should not use that reality as an excuse to avoid learning what matters to the person they love.

Communication is not simply telling someone what we want once. It involves creating an ongoing understanding of what matters to both people and recognizing when those needs change.

Sometimes people do not communicate an expectation because they want to see whether the other person will recognize it independently.

They may believe that asking for something takes away its meaning. If they have to request affection, attention, reassurance, or consideration, they may feel that the gesture is no longer genuine.

They want the other person to think of it on their own.

There is an understandable desire behind that feeling. Most people enjoy being remembered without having to ask. A thoughtful gesture can feel especially meaningful when someone has taken the initiative because they know it matters.

But there is a difference between appreciating thoughtfulness and turning unspoken expectations into tests.

A person may intentionally remain quiet about what they want, waiting to see whether their partner notices. If the partner does not recognize the expectation, the person may interpret the result as evidence that they are not important.

The partner may never realize that a test was taking place.

This can create disappointment without giving either person a fair opportunity to understand what happened.

If something matters to us, we should be willing to communicate it. That does not mean every act of kindness must be requested or that thoughtfulness has no value. It means we should not make another person’s ability to guess our expectations the measurement of their love.

Sometimes the most honest thing we can say is, “It would mean a lot to me if you planned something for us occasionally. I enjoy knowing that you have thought about spending time together.”

That statement gives the other person something meaningful to understand and an opportunity to respond.

If the request is repeatedly ignored after it has been clearly communicated, then the concern becomes different. It is no longer simply about an expectation that was never expressed. It becomes a question of whether the other person is willing to consider something they know matters.

Expectations can become especially difficult when one person believes they are making a sincere effort but the other person continues feeling disappointed.

The person making the effort may think about everything they have done. They may remember the time they arranged activities, adjusted their schedule, helped with responsibilities, or made sacrifices to keep the relationship moving forward.

From their perspective, those actions demonstrate care.

The other person may appreciate those efforts but still want something different. Perhaps they want more conversation, affection, or emotional reassurance. Perhaps they want to feel that the other person is interested in their thoughts rather than simply sharing the same space.

The difficulty begins when each person assumes that their own understanding of effort should settle the matter.

One may say, “I do all these things for you, and it still isn’t enough.”

The other may respond, “I’m not saying you don’t do anything. I’m telling you what I need.”

Both statements reveal something important.

The first person may feel unappreciated, while the second feels unheard. One wants their efforts recognized, and the other wants their unmet needs acknowledged.

A healthy relationship must make room for both.

Recognizing someone’s effort does not mean that every need has been met. Expressing an unmet need does not mean that everything the other person has done is meaningless.

The conversation should not become a competition between what one person gives and what the other receives.

It should become an opportunity to understand what each person needs in order to experience the relationship as healthy and fulfilling.

Not all expectations begin within our own relationships.

Some come from what we observed growing up. We may have watched our parents, grandparents, relatives, or other couples and developed ideas about what love should look like.

Perhaps we grew up seeing one person take responsibility for planning everything. Maybe affection was openly expressed in our household, or perhaps love was demonstrated primarily through work, sacrifice, and providing for the family.

Those experiences can influence what we consider normal.

Someone who grew up in a household where disagreements were discussed openly may struggle to understand a partner who becomes quiet during conflict. Someone who grew up around emotional distance may not recognize that a partner’s request for affection is an important need rather than an unnecessary demand.

Neither person may realize that their expectations are connected to experiences they have carried for years.

We can also inherit expectations from previous relationships. We may become accustomed to a particular level of communication, a certain routine, or a way of receiving affection. When a new relationship develops differently, we may begin wondering why the other person does not behave the way we have come to expect.

But a relationship is not a continuation of someone else’s relationship.

The person we love deserves the opportunity to build something with us rather than constantly being measured against an arrangement that existed before they arrived.

That does not mean we should lower our standards for respect, honesty, and consideration. It means we should recognize which expectations represent healthy foundations and which ones are simply familiar habits we have never questioned.

There is an important distinction between an expectation that has never been expressed and a need that has been communicated repeatedly.

If someone has clearly explained that they need more meaningful time together, and the other person continues dismissing the concern, the issue is no longer simply a failure to communicate.

If someone has explained that certain words or behaviors are hurtful, and those behaviors continue without acknowledgment or effort to change, the concern deserves to be taken seriously.

Communication gives another person an opportunity to understand. It does not guarantee that they will respond with consideration.

This is where expectations become connected to boundaries.

A person can explain what they need, communicate why it matters, and allow the other person an opportunity to respond. But they cannot force someone to care, change, or participate in the relationship in the way they believe is necessary.

At some point, repeated behavior provides information.

We must be willing to recognize the difference between someone who does not understand what we are asking and someone who understands but is unwilling to make an effort.

That distinction can be difficult because it may require us to reconsider what we are expecting from the relationship and whether the other person is willing to meet us in the places that matter.

Understanding someone else’s circumstances should not require us to abandon our own needs.

Expectations can also become overwhelming when one person believes the other should be responsible for anticipating every emotional need.

A partner may be expected to recognize every change in mood, understand every disappointment, offer reassurance whenever uncertainty appears, and respond in exactly the right way during every difficult moment.

No matter how much someone cares, they will not always recognize what another person is feeling.

They may be distracted, tired, overwhelmed, or dealing with circumstances that have nothing to do with the relationship. They may make a mistake or respond poorly without realizing the effect their behavior has created.

That does not mean their actions should never be questioned. It means that a healthy relationship must allow room for human limitations.

We cannot expect another person to remove every uncertainty from our lives or guarantee that we will never feel disappointed.

We must also learn how to communicate our emotions, manage our own reactions, and recognize when our expectations have become unrealistic.

Love should provide support, not become a requirement that another person manage every part of our emotional experience.

The same is true in reverse. Someone cannot expect their partner to remain endlessly patient while their own behavior repeatedly creates frustration. Both people must recognize that consideration is a shared responsibility.

Many expectations become easier to understand when people finally discuss what they mean to one another.

A person might explain that they do not need expensive gifts or elaborate plans. They simply want their partner to occasionally take the initiative and create time together.

Someone else might explain that they are not avoiding conversation because they do not care. They may need time to process their thoughts before discussing something difficult.

One person may explain that frequent communication provides reassurance. The other may explain that constant messages throughout the day make it difficult to concentrate on work and responsibilities.

Those conversations may reveal differences that neither person fully recognized before.

They may also reveal opportunities for compromise.

Perhaps they can agree to set aside certain evenings for uninterrupted time together. Perhaps they can establish a way to communicate when one person needs space without leaving the other uncertain. Perhaps they can become more intentional about showing appreciation in ways that the other person recognizes.

The solution will not always be complicated, but it does require both people to participate.

The purpose of communicating expectations is not to create a list of rules that someone must follow perfectly. It is to help two people understand what makes their relationship meaningful and what needs attention.

Consider the expectations you carry into your relationships.

Have you clearly communicated what matters to you, or have you been waiting for someone to recognize it without being told? Do you become disappointed when someone fails to meet an expectation they may not even know exists?

Think about where those expectations originated. Did they develop through previous relationships, family experiences, personal preferences, or comparisons with other people? Are they connected to healthy needs, or are they based on assumptions about how someone should behave if they truly love you?

If someone has clearly communicated a need to you, consider whether you have made an honest effort to understand it. Have you dismissed it because it is different from what you personally need, or have you tried to find a reasonable way to respond?

Most importantly, ask whether your expectations leave room for two individuals to have different personalities, responsibilities, and emotional needs.

You should not have to beg for respect, honesty, or basic consideration. But you should be willing to communicate the specific ways you experience connection and allow the other person the opportunity to understand them.

Likewise, you should be willing to listen when someone explains that their needs are different from yours.

There is something important about finally expressing a need that has been sitting quietly beneath disappointment.

It allows another person to understand what we have been carrying. It replaces guessing with communication and gives both people a clearer opportunity to decide how they want to move forward.

Sometimes the other person will respond with understanding. Sometimes they will need time to recognize what the request means. Sometimes they may disagree, and the two people will have to determine whether a reasonable compromise is possible.

There will also be moments when someone communicates their needs clearly and discovers that the other person is unwilling or unable to respond in a meaningful way.

That realization can be painful, but it is better than spending years expecting someone to understand a conversation that has never taken place.

We cannot expect another person to read every thought, recognize every disappointment, or anticipate every desire. At the same time, we should not have to continually explain why honesty, respect, affection, and consideration matter.

The balance is found in communicating what we need while paying attention to how the other person responds.

A relationship becomes healthier when expectations are discussed rather than silently enforced, when differences are considered rather than automatically judged, and when both people understand that love requires more than assuming they already know what the other person wants.

Because sometimes the disappointment we carry is not simply about what someone failed to do. It is about the meaning we attached to their actions before giving them the opportunity to understand what we needed.

And once we begin expressing those needs honestly, we create an opportunity to hear something just as important in return: what the other person has been trying to tell us all along.
