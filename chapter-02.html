Chapter Two

The Things We Don’t Say

What remains unspoken can shape everything that follows.

Not every disagreement begins with an argument. Some begin with something a person wanted to say but decided to keep to themselves.

It may have been a question they were afraid to ask, a disappointment they did not know how to explain, or a feeling they hoped the other person would recognize without having to be told. Whatever it was, they chose to remain quiet. Perhaps they did not want to ruin the evening, start another disagreement, or make the other person feel as though nothing they did was ever good enough.

At first, silence can seem like the easier choice. It allows the moment to pass without confrontation, and both people can continue with their day as though nothing is wrong. Yet the feeling that was never expressed does not necessarily disappear. It can remain in the background, influencing the next conversation, the next interaction, and eventually the way one person begins to view the other.

There is a difference between choosing not to argue and learning how to communicate peacefully. One creates an opportunity for understanding, while the other can leave important feelings buried beneath an appearance of normalcy.

A person may say everything is fine because explaining what is wrong feels exhausting. Another may accept that answer because they genuinely believe the matter has been resolved. Both may walk away from the same conversation with completely different understandings of what happened.

The person who remained quiet may believe the other should have recognized their disappointment. The person who accepted the answer may believe there is nothing left to discuss. Neither may realize that the conversation they thought they had was never really finished.

The Words That Never Make It Out

Imagine two people sitting together after a long day. They have finally found time to relax, but one of them is carrying something that has been bothering them. They want to bring it up, although they are already anticipating how the conversation might go.

They begin thinking about how to word it. They wonder whether the other person will become defensive, whether their feelings will be dismissed, or whether the discussion will turn into another argument about something that happened days earlier.

They may even rehearse the conversation in their mind, imagining several possible responses before saying a single word.

Eventually, the opportunity passes.

The other person may ask whether anything is wrong, and the answer comes quickly: “No, everything is fine.”

But everything is not fine. The person simply does not know how to explain what they are feeling without opening a conversation they are not sure they can handle.

This is where silence begins to communicate something of its own, even when that was never the intention.

The person who remains quiet may believe they are protecting the relationship from another disagreement. The person sitting beside them may interpret that silence as distance, frustration, or a lack of interest. Without an honest explanation, each person is left to interpret what the other is doing.

The problem is not that people need to discuss every passing thought or emotion. Everyone needs time to process their feelings, and there are moments when waiting before responding is the wisest decision. The problem develops when postponing a conversation becomes a permanent way of avoiding it.

A relationship cannot resolve concerns that neither person is willing to acknowledge.

When Confrontation Feels Difficult

For some people, confrontation is uncomfortable because they do not know how to express themselves when emotions become involved. They may struggle to find the right words, become overwhelmed when someone raises their voice, or feel that they must defend every statement they make.

For others, confrontation brings back memories of previous experiences in which speaking honestly led to rejection, humiliation, anger, or punishment. They may have learned that keeping quiet was safer than expressing disagreement.

These experiences can influence how someone approaches conflict long after the original circumstances have changed.

A person may want to have an honest conversation but become anxious as soon as the subject is introduced. They may start explaining themselves too quickly, become emotional, withdraw from the discussion, or agree to something simply because they want the tension to end.

This does not mean they do not care about the relationship. In some cases, they care so much that the possibility of another disagreement feels overwhelming.

However, avoiding confrontation does not automatically protect a relationship. Important concerns still need attention, and difficult conversations cannot always be postponed without consequences.

Learning to communicate does not mean becoming comfortable with every disagreement. It means developing the ability to express concerns without attacking another person, while also learning to remain present when someone else expresses something that may be difficult to hear.

Both people have a responsibility to create an environment where honesty is possible. One person should not have to fear being punished for speaking, and the other should not have to feel that every conversation is an accusation.

The Expectation That Someone Should Just Know

One of the most common sources of frustration in relationships is the expectation that someone should understand what we need without being told.

We may believe that if someone truly loves us, they should recognize when something is bothering us. We may expect them to notice when we are quieter than usual, understand why a particular comment hurt, or realize that we need reassurance without having to ask for it.

Sometimes people do notice. They may recognize a change in someone’s mood or realize that something has happened. But recognizing that something is wrong is not the same as understanding exactly what the other person needs.

People are not mind readers, even when they know each other well.

One person may express affection through physical closeness, while the other feels most connected through conversation. Someone may believe that making plans and spending time together demonstrate commitment, while their partner may need more verbal reassurance to feel emotionally secure. Both may be showing love, yet neither may fully understand how the other experiences it.

When those differences are never discussed, expectations begin replacing communication.

A person may become disappointed because their partner did not do something they never clearly requested. The partner may become frustrated because they are being criticized for failing to meet an expectation they did not know existed.

Eventually, the conversation becomes less about understanding what each person needs and more about defending why each person believes they have done enough.

That is a difficult place for any relationship to remain.

It is reasonable to want someone to pay attention, notice changes, and show consideration without being reminded about every little thing. Thoughtfulness matters. Consistency matters. But expecting someone to understand every unspoken need creates a standard that neither person can reliably meet.

Sometimes the most loving thing we can do is explain what matters to us rather than waiting for someone to discover it through trial and error.

When Silence Becomes a Message

Silence can mean many different things, and assuming that every quiet moment carries the same meaning can create unnecessary hurt.

Someone may become quiet because they are angry. Another may withdraw because they are overwhelmed. Someone else may need time to organize their thoughts before attempting a difficult conversation. There are also times when silence is used deliberately to punish, intimidate, or make another person feel responsible for restoring peace.

Those experiences should not be treated as though they are interchangeable.

Taking time to calm down can be healthy when a person communicates that they need a break and intends to return to the conversation. Refusing to communicate indefinitely, using silence to create fear, or withholding affection to control another person’s response is something different.

The difference often becomes clear through what happens afterward.

Does the person return willing to talk? Can both people explain what they experienced? Is there an effort to resolve the disagreement, or does one person remain responsible for guessing what went wrong and finding a way to make everything right?

A healthy relationship must allow room for emotional space without turning that space into punishment.

It is possible to need quiet without abandoning communication. It is possible to disagree without withdrawing love, and it is possible to take responsibility for one’s emotions without making another person responsible for managing them.

The challenge is learning how to communicate even when the words are difficult.

What Happens When Both People Keep Their Feelings to Themselves?

Sometimes both people are holding back.

One may be afraid that expressing disappointment will create another argument. The other may be afraid that admitting uncertainty will be interpreted as weakness or a lack of commitment. Both may be waiting for the other person to make the first move, hoping that time will somehow resolve what they have not been able to discuss.

From the outside, their relationship may appear perfectly ordinary. They continue spending time together, making plans, handling responsibilities, and talking about the future. Yet underneath those ordinary routines, there may be questions neither person has found the courage or opportunity to ask.

One may wonder whether they are truly understood. The other may wonder why their efforts never seem to be enough. One may feel emotionally alone, while the other may feel that nothing they do is appreciated.

Neither person may realize how much the other is carrying.

This is why listening matters just as much as speaking. A person may finally find the courage to express something they have been holding inside, only to discover that the other person is already preparing a defense. The conversation then becomes a competition between explanations instead of an opportunity to understand.

Sometimes the most important response is not an immediate explanation. It is the willingness to listen long enough to understand why something matters to the person saying it.

That does not mean agreeing with every accusation or accepting responsibility for something you did not do. It means allowing another person to finish expressing themselves before deciding how to respond.

The same consideration must be returned. Listening cannot become a one-sided responsibility in which one person is always expected to understand while the other is never expected to explain.

Both people deserve to be heard.

Learning to Say What Needs to Be Said

Honest communication requires more than finding the right words. It requires a willingness to be vulnerable enough to express what is happening internally while recognizing that the other person may have a different experience.

Instead of allowing frustration to build until it becomes an accusation, a person can begin by explaining what they have noticed and how it has affected them. They can ask questions rather than immediately assigning motives. They can express disappointment without attacking someone’s character.

Likewise, the person receiving that concern can learn to pause before responding defensively. They can ask what the other person needs, explain their own perspective, and acknowledge the impact of their actions even when the impact was not intentional.

There will still be difficult conversations. There will be moments when two people disagree, misunderstand one another, or discover that their expectations are not aligned. Communication does not eliminate every conflict, but it gives people a better opportunity to address problems before those problems become deeply rooted.

There must also be room for honesty about behavior that cannot simply be explained away. If someone repeatedly dismisses concerns, refuses accountability, or uses intimidation to prevent difficult conversations, the solution is not for the other person to find increasingly careful words. Healthy communication requires safety and mutual respect.

The goal is not to make every conversation comfortable. The goal is to make honest conversations possible.

Looking Beneath the Surface

Before leaving an important conversation unfinished, consider what you are afraid might happen if you speak honestly. Are you avoiding the discussion because you need time to process your emotions, or because you have become convinced that your feelings will never matter? Are you expecting someone to recognize an unspoken need, or have you given them a genuine opportunity to understand what you are asking for?

If someone approaches you with a concern, consider whether you are listening to understand or simply preparing to defend yourself. Ask whether your response addresses the feeling being expressed or only the words that made you uncomfortable.

Most importantly, recognize that silence does not always mean peace, and speaking does not automatically mean conflict. Sometimes the conversation we keep avoiding is the very conversation that could help us understand one another.

Not every relationship can be repaired through communication alone, and not every disagreement has a simple resolution. However, two people cannot build understanding while continually withholding the information each needs to understand the other.

The things we do not say can become the things we eventually argue about. They can turn into assumptions, resentment, emotional distance, and questions that grow harder to answer with every passing day.

Perhaps the conversation does not need to begin perfectly. Perhaps it simply needs to begin honestly.

Because when we finally say what we have been holding inside, we give the other person an opportunity to hear something they may never have understood otherwise.

And sometimes, that is where understanding finally begins.
